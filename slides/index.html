05Method: the rule

Price each action for this payment, then take the cheapest

Every model here gives a fraud score p, between zero and one. The rule turns p and the amount into an action.

approve costsp × amount

what we expect to lose if it is fraud

review costsfee + λ

{{costs.review_fee|num}} USD, plus λ: a price for using up one of the few reviews

decline costs(1 − p) × friction

friction: {{costs.friction_rate|pct}} of the amount, at least {{costs.friction_min|num}} USD

[Swan · 0:45] Before the models, the one piece every model shares: the rule that turns a score into an action. Every model in this talk gives a fraud score p between zero and one. The rule prices each action for this one payment and takes the cheapest. Approving costs p times the amount: what we expect to lose. Declining costs one minus p, times the friction. A review costs its {{costs.review_fee|num}} dollar fee plus lambda, a price for using up one of the few reviews. Our transfer of {{worked.amount|usd2}} dollars, with p {{illustration.rule.p|d2}}, the score logistic regression gives it on the next slide (made-up weights): approving would expect to lose {{illustration.rule.big.approve|usd2}}, declining {{illustration.rule.big.decline|usd2}}, a review {{illustration.rule.review|num}}. So: review. Click: the same p on a {{illustration.rule.small.amount|num}} dollar payment. Now a review costs more than the risk, so the rule declines. Same score, different action: the amount decides. Two fixed cut-offs on the score, which is what we proposed at the midterm, cannot do that. Measured on {{rule.phase14.windows_n|num}} earlier test windows, on the same logistic-regression scores: the rule cost {{rule.phase14.diff.mean|abs}} dollars less per window than two fixed cut-offs, and less in {{rule.phase14.diff.first_cheaper|num}} of the {{rule.phase14.windows_n|num}} windows. The 95% interval of the per-window difference runs from {{rule.phase14.diff.ci.0|abs}} to {{rule.phase14.diff.ci.1|abs}} dollars saved; it stays on one side of zero, which is what we call distinguishable. The price, said out loud: it declined more good customers, {{manual.phase14.good_declined_rule|int}} against {{manual.phase14.good_declined_lines|int}}, because a wrong decline is priced at only a tenth of the amount. “yes” counts as one, “no” as zero.

Score → probability{{illustration.logreg.p|d2}}

p = 1 ÷ (1 + e−score): any score lands between zero and one

Each weight changes the odds of fraud

odds = p ÷ (1 − p). Being a transfer adds +{{illustration.logreg.rows.transfer.w|d2}} to the score, so it multiplies the odds by e{{illustration.logreg.rows.transfer.w|d2}} = {{illustration.logreg.odds_times|d2}}. A fact that is “no” changes nothing.

[Swan · 0:50] Logistic regression is the simplest real model we use. Each fact about the payment gets a weight. Multiply each fact by its weight and add them up: that is the score. Our transfer, with made-up weights: start at {{illustration.logreg.start|d2}}. It is a transfer, and yes counts as one, so add {{illustration.logreg.rows.transfer.adds|d2}}. Not at night, it is hour {{worked.hour}}, and not a round amount: those add nothing. The score is {{illustration.logreg.score|d2}}. The sigmoid squashes any score into a number between zero and one: here {{illustration.logreg.p|d2}}. That p goes into the rule from the last slide. Click: how to read a weight. Odds are p over one minus p.
