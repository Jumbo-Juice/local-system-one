Price each action for this payment, then take the cheapest

[Swan · 0:45] Before the models, the one piece every model shares: the rule that turns a score into an action. Every model in this talk gives a fraud score p between zero and one. The rule prices each action for this one payment and takes the cheapest. Approving costs p times the amount: what we expect to lose. Declining costs one minus p, times the friction. A review costs its {{costs.review_fee|num}} dollar fee plus lambda, a price for using up one of the few reviews. Our transfer of {{worked.amount|usd2}} dollars, with p {{illustration.rule.p|d2}}, the score logistic regression gives it on the next slide (made-up weights): approving would expect to lose {{illustration.rule.big.approve|usd2}}, declining {{illustration.rule.big.decline|usd2}}, a review {{illustration.rule.review|num}}. So: review. Click: the same p on a {{illustration.rule.small.amount|num}} dollar payment. Now a review costs more than the risk, so the rule declines. Same score, different action: the amount decides. Two fixed cut-offs on the score, which is what we proposed at the midterm, cannot do that. Measured on {{rule.phase14.windows_n|num}} earlier test windows, on the same logistic-regression scores: the rule cost {{rule.phase14.diff.mean|abs}} dollars less per window than two fixed cut-offs, and less in {{rule.phase14.diff.first_cheaper|num}} of the {{rule.phase14.windows_n|num}} windows. Model

{{paysim.model|short}} [10]

one forward pass, the text read once; we take the score of each letter as the next word [5]

Logits: log-scores, shifted so the top is 0

A logit is a model’s raw score for an answer, before it is a probability: higher is more likely.

Softmax: three probabilities that add up to one

Take elogit of each, divide by their sum: decline is {{worked.exp.listed.2|d2}} ÷ {{worked.exp.sum_listed|d2}} = {{worked.orders.listed.2|d2}}.

Softmax ignores a shift, so these are the logits up to a constant. A real PaySim payment (simulated data); the model is not yet trained.

[Juji · 1:15] Now the new part: a language model as the decision maker. It does not chat and it does not write. We give it our transfer as plain facts: a transfer of {{worked.amount|usd2}} dollars to a customer at hour {{worked.hour}}; the receiver is known, it was paid {{worked.facts.receiver_before}} times before; the amount was not seen earlier this hour; it is not round. Then one multiple-choice question: does this match the fraud pattern? A, no, approve it. B, unsure, send it to an analyst. C, yes, decline it. The model, Qwen 2.5 with 1.5 billion weights, reads all of that in one forward pass: the text goes through the network once. At the end it scores every possible next word. We read only three of those scores: the ones for A, B and C. Those scores are logits: raw scores, higher means more likely. Here they are, shifted so the top one is zero: A {{worked.logits.listed.0|d2}}, B {{worked.logits.listed.1|d2}}, C {{worked.logits.listed.2|d2}}. Click: softmax turns them into probabilities. Take e to each logit, and divide by the sum. e to zero is one; e to {{worked.logits.listed.0|d2}} is about {{worked.exp.listed.0|d2}}. So decline gets {{worked.exp.listed.2|d2}} over {{worked.exp.sum_listed|d2}}, which is {{worked.orders.listed.2|d2}}; approve and review get {{worked.orders.listed.0|d2}} each. Adding the same number to every logit changes nothing after softmax, which is why we can shift them so the top is zero. So the language model becomes a classifier: one pass, three probabilities, and it never writes a word. That is the idea from System One models, reference 13. 14Biases

Two biases built in: prices we assumed, and a lean towards a position

Assumed costs assumed

Every dollar here rests on prices we chose, not measured: a wrong decline {{costs.friction_rate|pct}} of the amount, at least {{costs.friction_min|num}} USD; a review {{costs.review_fee|num}} USD.

Change one price

Charge a flat price for each good customer declined. At {{manual.stress.flat_low}}–{{manual.stress.flat_high}} USD a customer, the rule’s mean saving over two fixed cut-offs (slide 05) becomes a loss; from {{manual.stress.not_distinguishable_from}} USD it is already not distinguishable.

Option-order bias [6]

The untrained PaySim model leans towards a letter’s position, not only its meaning. Our transfer (slide 09): listed, top answer {{worked.top.listed}}; reversed, {{worked.top.reversed}}. The last letter both times.

{{paysim.order_flips}} of {{paysim.model_n}}

PaySim payments in that window whose top answer flipped with the order. On PaySim we averaged both orders. On real payments the trained model read one order, chosen on dev; its lean there was not measured.

15Takeaways

A language model can decide in one pass. On real payments, it showed no distinguishable saving.

What holds
  • A language model reads as a classifier: one pass, one score per letter, no words.
  • A small adapter took it from useless to matching the hand-written rules on PaySim.
  • The language model alone took {{final.latency.p90|int}} ms for {{manual.conventions.latency_quantile}}% of payments, against a {{final.latency.bar|int}} ms bar; the whole stack was not timed.
  • Far fewer good customers declined, mostly thanks to the customer line.
What doesn’t, or wasn’t checked
  • No setup showed a distinguishable saving against logistic regression on the final test.
  • The cheapest-looking language setup mostly copies the tree score.
  • The prices are assumed, and the untrained PaySim model leaned towards the last letter.
  • Fairness was not measured: who gets declined was never checked by group.

The paper and the analyst console have the details.

What doesn't: no setup showed a distinguishable saving against logistic regression on the final test; the cheapest-looking language setup mostly copies the tree score; the prices are assumed, and the untrained PaySim model leaned towards the last letter; and fairness was not measured.
