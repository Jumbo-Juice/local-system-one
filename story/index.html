Can a model that never writes be trusted to decide?

Most AI tools think by writing. They build an answer one word at a time. This one never writes. It is handed a question with lettered options, takes one look, and we read which letter it leans to.

We put that idea through six trials, in three games and on two sets of payments, one simulated and one real. Each trial ends on a stamp: yes, no, only with help or can’t tell.

Nothing on this page runs a model. Every figure is a recorded run, or arithmetic on results whose rules were written down before the results were known.

Trial 2. Can it be trusted with one question?

Each look gives every option a score. It is tempting to read a top score of 0.99 as “99% sure”.

We checked on questions with known answers. One model’s top score averaged in 100 while it was right times in 100. Every model we tried leaned the same way.

So a score ranks the options. It is not odds.

Hand it a long list and it loses its place. With 10 options in one question it found the right one of the time. With 80 it managed .

The help is a tournament: split the options into groups of 10, take one look at each group, and run the winners against each other. With 80 options that kept it at , for about twice the time.

The order of the options matters more than it should.

On a short, clear question it chooses well. To stay that way it needed tournaments for long lists and a second look in the other order. Its scores can’t be read as odds at all.

Trial 3. Can it be trusted with a whole game?

In the shooter, one agent clears locked rooms, takes the key and leaves by the exit. Nothing is scripted. On every tick the model answers a goal question, a target question, a move question and a shoot question.

This is a recorded run of the larger model. We chose which run to show before we knew how it would end.

The blue dot is the agent. Red shapes are enemies, hollow while asleep, and the small red dots are their bullets. The key is orange and the exit green.

The model chooses “”, with a score of .

It keeps doing it. times in this run it steps into a labelled bullet path while a safe move is on the list.

The red marks under the map are those steps. On tick a gunner kills it, key in hand.

That run was the rule, not the exception. Nearly every hit either model took came from a move it had been told was dangerous, chosen while a safe one was offered.

Spelling the danger out more clearly changed nothing.

A second problem only shows over time. The model gives the same answer every time it meets the same moment. So one wrong answer in one spot repeats, every time it comes back there.

A score card of single questions can’t see that. Only playing the whole game can.

What fixed the games was structure around the model, not a better model.

Layers of decisions came first: a slow goal, then a target, then a move. In the grid game that gave times the gems and times the food. Choosing moves alone did no better than random.

Then two rules: never offer a dangerous move when a safe one exists, and keep a target until you reach it.

With that help, both models escaped of maps in the final checks: in the shooter, and in a dungeon where the agent has no weapon and must slip past ghouls.

Scroll to play one of those runs: the blue dot again, with ghouls in red. An earlier dungeon, with corridors one cell wide, was escaped times in before we rebuilt it.

Stamped only with help

The escapes show the model can plan and find its way. They do not show that it judges danger: the safety comes from the list of options we hand it.

Trial 4. Can it be trusted with a payment, as it comes?

The serious test is fraud. Every payment gets one of three actions: approve, review (hand it to a person) or decline.

A few hand-written rules approve the kinds of payment that never had fraud. The model judges the rest, one look each. We began on PaySim, a public set of simulated mobile-money payments.

Here is one recorded decision. The payment was genuine.

It did that every time. With no training, the model declined all payments it judged in the test: frauds, and genuine payments.

Its scores were not noise. They put a fraud above a genuine payment about times in 100. But its top answer never moved off “decline”, however we worded the question.

Six hand-written rules cost less in every one of the test windows. A window is a block of payments in the order they arrived.

Stamped no

As it comes, the model can’t be trusted with a payment. It declines everything.

Trial 5. Can it be trusted after training?

So we trained it: minutes on the laptop, on earlier payments with known answers.

The trained model wanted the same action as the rule book on of decisions. It was cheaper than logistic regression in of new windows.

It never beat the rules: of . In this simulated data two clues give almost every fraud away, so little is left for a model to add.

Simulated payments were too easy, so we moved to real ones: card payments from a public competition, split by time.

Cost is now in dollars: fraud let through, good customers declined, and the fee for each review. These costs are assumed, not measured.

Training paid again.

Part of that gain is not the model at all. It is the rule that turns a score into an action.

A missed fraud costs its whole amount. Declining a good customer costs a tenth of it. A review costs a flat fee. So the cheapest action depends on the amount.

Try it: move the two sliders and watch the cheapest action change.

On real payments, that rule alone cut the cost with the scores left untouched.

It is arithmetic around the model, and it works on any scores.

Stamped only with help

Training turned a model that declined everything into one that wanted the rule book’s action on nearly every payment. On practice windows its cost was not distinguishable from logistic regression’s. Training and the cost rule did that. It was never cheaper than the rules.

It is quick enough to decide in real time. But every gain we measured came from the structure around the model, or from training it.

None came from its judgement as it comes. The structure was layers of decisions, lists of options, a second look in the other order, tournaments, and a rule that weighs the cost. With all of that help its cost was not distinguishable from logistic regression’s, and it was never cheaper than the hand-written rules. This reading is ours, drawn from the six trials; it was not itself a test.

What this can’t tell you

  • The costs are assumed. Nobody measured what a wrongly declined customer or a review really costs. Every dollar figure here rests on those assumptions.
  • The final test is spent. It ran once. It can’t be run again to settle “can’t tell”, and any new claim about it would be a second look.
  • One training run per setup. On practice windows a second run with another seed moved the cost by about a window, which is the size of some differences we were trying to measure.
  • A cheaper setup that we don’t count. When the model also read a score from decision trees, it cost less. But its answers followed that score almost exactly (a rank match of ), so it is not the model’s own judgement.
  • The game samples are small. Ten or twenty runs decide little. Only wide gaps held up when we repeated them.
  • The games are beatable by design. Their safety comes from the option lists. The escapes show planning, not a sense of danger.
  • Simulated payments are not real ones. PaySim is generated data, and two clues give away almost every fraud in it.
  • Fairness was not measured. We did not check whether mistakes fall evenly across kinds of customers.

Sources

The idea

The idea comes from Jev, a commercial model described by TypeSafe, and from Sean Goedecke’s articles about it. This project is not Jev and does not reproduce it: we rebuilt only the publicly described trick, on open models.

  • Sean Goedecke, Two techniques for working with System One models
  • Sean Goedecke, Jev means structured output is interesting again
  • TypeSafe, Introducing System One models and Jev

Models and data

  • Qwen2.5 with 1.5 billion and 3 billion parameters, for the games and the simulated payments. d1-Omni-2B for the real payments.
  • PaySim, by E. A. Lopez-Rojas, A. Elmir and S. Axelsson: simulated mobile-money payments, CC BY-SA 4.0.
  • IEEE-CIS Fraud Detection: real card payments from a public competition. No payment from it appears on this page, only results per window.
